# Sentence composition

Read when explaining, composing or correcting a sentence. Start from intended meaning, speaker/addressee and context rather than substituting words into English syntax.

## Working foundations

- A basic transitive clause commonly orders subject–verb–object; an intransitive verb need not take an object. `Ó ra bàtà.` means “He/she bought shoes.” Pronouns do not supply English-style grammatical gender. [1]
- Many noun modifiers follow the noun: `ilé pupa` means “red house.” Do not generalize this to every modifier: plural `àwọn` precedes a noun, and context can already establish plurality. [1,2]
- Subject, object, possessive and emphatic pronouns are not freely interchangeable. Check their form and tone in context; respect and social relationship can affect address. [2]
- Verbs do not take English-style person/tense endings. Particles, aspect and context contribute to time reference; a bare verb is not automatically a past tense. Progressive `ń`, future/habitual constructions and negation should be taught in their actual clause pattern, not as one-to-one English auxiliaries. [1]

## Negation and questions

`kò` precedes the verb in a basic negative clause: `Bọ́lá kò mu omi.` (“Bọ́lá is not drinking water” in the lesson context). Do not negate every construction by inserting the same particle: pronoun and aspect patterns can change.

`Ṣé` introduces a yes/no question; `Kí ni` asks for “what” in the illustrated construction. Contrast `Ṣé o fẹ́ jẹun?` (“Do you want to eat?”) with a request for missing information. These are starting patterns, not all Yorùbá question types. [3]

## Composition and correction

Identify who does what to whom, and whether the sentence asserts, asks, commands, denies, focuses or reports. Check the relevant pattern before extending a basic SVO example to negation, questions, serial verbs, relative clauses or focus. Use source-backed examples for a new construction rather than inventing a complete paradigm from analogy.

Preserve idiom, register and intended meaning. Distinguish grammatical correction from an optional stylistic alternative. Keep word boundaries and punctuation readable; do not join words or add apostrophes merely to imitate rapid speech. Verify established contractions and compound spellings where they matter.

Return the corrected or composed sentence, a natural translation when helpful, and the smallest explanation that makes the construction reusable. A word-by-word gloss can expose structure but is not the final idiomatic translation.
